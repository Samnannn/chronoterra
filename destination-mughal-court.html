The journey

Agra in 1631 is a city at the crest of a wave. The Mughal court of Shah Jahan is rebuilding its fort in luminous white marble, its gardens follow the fourfold charbagh plan, and its craftsmen are perfecting the inlay of semi-precious stones into stone, a technique the empire will be remembered for.

Your expedition is arranged around observation, not participation. From a leased riverside house you will watch the machinery of a great court at work: the ordered galleries, the scent of rosewater, the hierarchy of who stands where and for how long. A licensed guide-historian will teach you the salutations that keep visitors out of trouble.

You will not meet the emperor. The Board guarantees nothing of the kind, and neither does history.

Highlights

  1. A court etiquette tutorial, including the formal salutations
  2. Gallery seating at a public audience, at a respectful distance
  3. An evening in a walled charbagh garden, with Persian verse
  4. Visits to marble-working ateliers under imperial patronage

Fact & fiction

What we know. What we imagine.

Rooted in the historical record. Daily detail is interpretive. We keep the two columns separate on purpose.

The record

  • Shah Jahan reigned from 1628 to 1658. Agra was the imperial capital until the court moved to Shahjahanabad (Delhi) in 1648.
  • In 1631 Empress Mumtaz Mahal died, and Shah Jahan commissioned the Taj Mahal as her mausoleum. Construction began around 1632 and took roughly two decades.
  • Court architecture combined Persianate, Timurid and Indian traditions, with red sandstone gradually giving way to white marble under Shah Jahan.

The reconstruction

  • Your hosts, guide and the specific events you witness are invented for the Board’s itinerary.
  • Conversation is imagined. Real individuals appear only as the historical record allows, and never speak for themselves in our scenes.

Sample itinerary

The shape of your stay.

A representative plan. Final timings are fixed at your pre-departure briefing, and the Board reserves the right to be surprised.

  1. Arrival

    The riverside house

    Emerge at a Board-leased house on the Yamuna. Fitting for period dress. Return coordinate checked, double-checked, and sewn into your hem.

  2. Etiquette

    The salutation lesson

    Your guide-historian teaches you the taslim and kornish, and how to avoid being noticed doing either badly.

  3. The court

    A public audience

    Observe a great audience from the visitors’ gallery. Count the ranks. Do not point.

  4. Evening

    Garden & verse

    Rosewater, lamps and recitation in a walled garden as the heat of the day breaks.

  5. Departure

    The return gate

    Dawn along the river. Your return coordinate is verified. Please leave nothing behind, including your sandals.

ADV-01Fictional advisory

Verify your return coordinate before departure.

Read it aloud. Read it again. Have your guide read it. A return coordinate that is correct to six decimal places is a promise; one that is correct to five is a story you will tell at dinner parties, if you get home.

ADV-07Fictional advisory

Do not remove artifacts from protected historical sites.

Not a coin. Not a pebble. Not a fragment of mosaic. Anything you carry out of an era will, at best, be confiscated at the gate and, at worst, be discovered in a museum with your fingerprints on it.

ADV-10Fictional advisory

Never disclose future events to historical figures.
